# CTCI I–II: The Interview Process + Behind the Scenes

## Chapter I: The Interview Process

### What they score you on
- Analytical skills: how you work through a problem, how much help you need, how good your solution is
- Coding skills: clean, well-organised code that turns the algorithm into something that works
- Technical knowledge: CS fundamentals, data structures and algorithms
- Experience: past projects, technical decisions you've made
- Culture fit and communication: how you explain your thinking and work with the interviewer

### Why they interview like this
- **False negatives are acceptable.** They'd rather reject a good engineer than hire a bad one. A bad hire is expensive, and there are plenty of other candidates.
- **Problem-solving matters.** Solving a problem you haven't seen before is a strong sign you'll be a good engineer.
- **DS&A knowledge is useful.** It drives real decisions in code, and it's easy to test.
- **Whiteboards keep it on the thinking.** No compiler or autocomplete, so they see how you reason instead of how well you remember syntax.

### How questions get picked
- Most companies have no approved question list. Each interviewer picks their own.
- So questions feel random, but big tech companies all ask the same kinds of questions.

### Scored relative to others
- You're compared with other candidates who got the same question, not against a fixed bar.
- A hard question doesn't mean you're failing. If you struggled, everyone else probably did too.

### FAQ
- Not hearing back doesn't mean a rejection. Usually the feedback hasn't reached the recruiter yet.
- You can reapply to the same company, usually after 6 to 12 months.

## Chapter II: Behind the Scenes

### Most companies run the same basic process
- Recruiter contact, then one or two phone screens, usually with an engineer
- On-site loop of about 4 to 6 interviews
- A lunch interview is often there to help you relax and usually isn't scored
- Interviewers write up feedback and either meet to discuss it or hand it to a committee
- Expect to hear back within about a week. Following up politely with the recruiter is fine.

### Microsoft
- You interview with a specific team
- 4 to 5 interviews on the day
- Being sent to the "as appropriate" interviewer, usually the hiring manager, at the end is a good sign
- Be ready to say why Microsoft and show you care about their products

### Amazon
- Usually one or two phone screens first
- On-site of 4 to 5 interviewers, often from different teams
- One of them is the **bar raiser**: from another team, specially trained, harder questions, and can veto the hire
- Expect a focus on scalability and object-oriented design
- Interviewers meet afterwards to decide

### Google
- Phone screen with an engineer, then 4 to 6 on-site interviews, sometimes with an unscored lunch
- Feedback covers analytical ability, coding, experience and communication, scored from 1.0 to 4.0
- A **hiring committee** that never met you makes the decision from the written feedback, then there are more reviews after that
- Expect scalability and algorithm questions

### Apple
- Little bureaucracy. Recruiter screen, then technical screens with the team.
- On-site with team members, often including lunch
- Interviewers compare notes during the day. If it's going well, you meet the director and then the VP.
- Know the product you'd be working on

### Facebook
- One or two phone screens, sometimes a take-home
- On-site interviewers play set roles: behavioural ("Jedi"), coding and algorithms ("Ninja"), design and architecture ("Pirate")
- You join a bootcamp after you're hired and pick a team later, so you aren't interviewing for one team
- Values moving fast and an entrepreneurial attitude

### Palantir
- You interview for a specific team
- Phone screens, sometimes a HackerRank coding challenge
- Up to 5 on-site interviews, with questions often harder than at other companies
